# The Fox and the Axolotl

Rowan the fox was the cleverest creature on the hill, and he knew it. He had outwitted three farmers, two hounds, and one very suspicious goose.

What he had never outwitted was the dry summer. By August the streams had shrunk to threads, and Rowan's belly was as empty as his pockets would have been, had foxes worn pockets.

One dusk, following a rumor of water, he found the old garden pond. A pale pink axolotl named Olo floated beneath the lily pads, smiling his permanent smile.

"Good evening," said Rowan, in his smoothest voice. "I'm told this pond has fish. Fat ones. Tell me where they hide, and I'll be on my way."

"There are some," said Olo. "But they're shy, and you're very hungry. Hungry foxes make poor fishers. Sit down. I'll show you."

Rowan, suspicious, sat. He had expected a trick. Tricks were what clever creatures were for.

Olo swam to the shallows and nudged a reed. "The fish gather here when the moon rises, because the water is cool. Wait quietly and keep your paw low."

Rowan waited. His paw twitched, his stomach grumbled, and he nearly gave up twice. Each time, Olo said, "Not yet," in such a calm voice that Rowan stayed.

When the moon rose, the fish came. Rowan caught one, then another, and ate until the hollow ache was gone.

"Why did you help me?" he asked at last. "I could have eaten *you*."

"You could have tried," said Olo mildly. "But you looked tired, and I like company." His smile didn't change, because it never did, but his gills fluttered warmly.

Rowan, who had outwitted everyone, found he had nothing clever to say.

So he said, "Thank you," which was harder.

After that, the fox came back every evening. He brought blackberries Olo couldn't eat and stories Olo loved, and Olo taught him the patience of still water. Rowan never did become less clever. But he became a good deal kinder, and the hill noticed.

*The End*
